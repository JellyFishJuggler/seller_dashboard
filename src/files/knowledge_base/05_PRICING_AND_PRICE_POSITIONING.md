# Pricing and Price Positioning

This document explains how a marketplace seller should think about price: where to position, how demand responds, when to raise or cut price, and how to test. All numbers are illustrative assumptions. Competitor prices are never assumed to be known; they are only used if the seller or a tool supplies them. Pricing rules, caps or fee structures of any specific platform must be confirmed from that platform (see KB-24).

## 1. Price positioning

Price positioning is where a product sits relative to comparable alternatives: budget, mid-market, or premium. Positioning must be matched by what the buyer sees: images, content, ratings, delivery promise and brand trust. A premium price with weak content converts poorly; a budget price with poor quality returns heavily.

Common positioning choices:
- **Low-price leader**: wins on price, needs low costs and high volume; thin margins, price wars.
- **Value positioning**: competitive price with clearly better quality or content.
- **Premium positioning**: higher price justified by differentiation (design, material, brand, warranty).

A KAM should ask what the seller's product is actually better or worse at, before suggesting any price move.

## 2. Value-based pricing and differentiation

Value-based pricing starts from what the buyer values, not from cost. A copper water bottle with a clear health-use description and good photos may justify more than a generic unbranded bottle. Differentiators: material, durability, design, bundle contents, warranty, size range, packaging, delivery speed.

If a product has no differentiator, price becomes the only lever, and margins shrink.

## 3. Price elasticity of demand

**Formula:** Price elasticity = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price.

- If the absolute value is greater than 1, demand is **elastic** (orders react strongly to price).
- If less than 1, demand is **inelastic** (orders react weakly).

**Example (illustrative):** Price falls from ₹500 to ₹450 (−10%); orders rise from 100 to 115 (+15%). Elasticity = 15 ÷ −10 = −1.5, elastic.

Revenue: before 500 × 100 = ₹50,000; after 450 × 115 = ₹51,750 (+3.5%).

Profit with product cost ₹300 (other costs ignored): before (500−300) × 100 = ₹20,000; after (450−300) × 115 = ₹17,250 (−13.75%). **Revenue rose but profit fell**, even with elastic demand. Elasticity above 1 does not alone justify a price cut.

Practical caution: a seller rarely observes clean elasticity because other things change at the same time (stock, ads, season). Treat measured elasticity as a rough hint.

## 4. Break-even volume for a price cut

If margin per unit is M and a cut reduces it to M′, orders must rise by (M ÷ M′ − 1) just to keep profit equal.

Example: price ₹500, cost ₹300, margin ₹200. Cut 10% to ₹450: margin ₹150. Required order growth = 200 ÷ 150 − 1 = 33.3%. A 15% order rise is not enough. A 20% cut to ₹400: margin ₹100; required rise = 100%.

Include per-order costs and price-linked fees in M for realism (KB-06, KB-07).

## 5. When to raise a price

- Stock is limited and demand exceeds supply; raising price can protect availability and margin.
- Product margin is thin and conversion is healthy and reviews are strong.
- Costs rose and the product has differentiators.

Example (illustrative): price ₹500 to ₹550 (+10%), cost ₹300; orders 100 to 92. Profit before 200 × 100 = 20,000; after 250 × 92 = 23,000 (+15%) while revenue 50,600. Orders lost 8% but profit rose. Watch conversion, ratings and returns after the change.

## 6. When to reduce a price

A price cut is justified when:
- Evidence shows the product is priced above alternatives that buyers see as equivalent (needs price comparison data) and conversion is low while clicks are healthy.
- The product is slow moving or seasonal stock at risk of becoming dead stock, and the cost of holding it exceeds margin lost.
- The break-even order lift is realistic given stock and demand.

A cut is **not** justified when:
- ASP is unchanged and the sales fall comes from stockouts, cancellations or visibility (price is not the bottleneck).
- The product is out of stock (a cut cannot create sales).
- Margins are already thin and the break-even lift is unrealistic.
- The cause of low conversion is content mismatch, poor images or reviews.

## 7. Risks of excessive discounting

- Margin erosion and the risk of selling below the minimum viable price (KB-06).
- Training buyers to wait for discounts.
- Attracting bargain-driven buyers with higher return and cancellation behaviour.
- Cannibalising full-price sales.
- Quality perception damage.
- Stock burn: a discount can create demand the seller cannot fulfil, causing cancellations.

One third-party news report mentioned a past marketplace communication about returns and deeply discounted products not meeting expectations; its current relevance is unverified.

## 8. Interpreting ASP changes

- ASP up with orders flat: price increase or premium mix.
- ASP down with orders up: discounts or cheaper mix.
- ASP flat but sales down: volume problem (the sample seller pattern, sales and orders both −32.5%, ASP unchanged at ₹500).
- ASP can hide product-level changes: some prices up, others down, offsetting. Check product-level prices.

## 9. Price testing methodology

1. Pick one product with steady demand and stock.
2. Record baseline for at least 1-2 weeks: daily orders, clicks, conversion, returns.
3. Change only price (not title, images or promotion at the same time).
4. Run long enough to cover normal weekly cycles; avoid festival days.
5. Compare orders, conversion, margin per order and returns, not revenue alone.
6. Keep stock sufficient during the test.
7. Roll back if margin falls and volume does not compensate.

Small tests on a few products limit risk. A/B testing is not always possible on a marketplace; sequential before/after tests are weaker evidence and should be described as such.

## 10. Pricing recommendations: when justified and when not

Justified: backed by product-level evidence (conversion, price gap, margin, stock level) and a break-even calculation.
Not justified: based only on a seller-level sales fall, a competitor price the seller cannot verify, or a hunch.

## 11. Questions a KAM should ask

- Did you change any price last month? Which products?
- What does your cost plus fees plus shipping come to per order on your top products?
- Do you know of similar products selling at lower prices?
- Has the same product sold at the current price before?
- Do you have enough stock to handle extra orders if you cut price?

## 12. What not to conclude

- Do not say "your price is too high" without comparison evidence.
- Do not say a discount will lift sales by a stated percentage.
- Do not state a platform price cap or fee rule; confirm via KB-24 and seller support.
