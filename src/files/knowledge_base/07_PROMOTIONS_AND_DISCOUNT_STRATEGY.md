# Promotions and Discount Strategy

This document explains when a promotion helps a marketplace seller, how to calculate whether it pays, and when to avoid it. Figures are illustrative assumptions. Platform-specific promotion tools, eligibility, discount caps or fees are not described here and must be confirmed from official platform documentation (KB-24).

## 1. Types of promotions

- **Price discount**: a lower selling price on a product.
- **Coupon or cart discount**: reduction applied at checkout, often on a minimum order value.
- **Bundling**: two or more products sold together at a combined price.
- **Volume offer**: lower unit price for more units (buy 2, save more).
- **Free or reduced shipping**: shipping cost absorbed by the seller.
- **Cross-sell and upsell**: suggesting complementary products (cross-sell) or a higher-priced version (upsell).
- **Clearance**: deep discount to move slow or dead stock.

## 2. When promotions are useful

- Clearing slow stock before it becomes dead or seasonal stock.
- Launching a new product that needs initial sales and feedback signals.
- Raising order value through bundles when the margin allows.
- Reaching sales during seasonal peaks when stock is sufficient.
- Competing where a price gap is verified to be the conversion barrier.

## 3. When to avoid promotions

- The product is out of stock or stock is low (demand cannot be fulfilled, so cancellations rise).
- Contribution margin per order is already thin (KB-06).
- The real problem is content, quality or delivery, not price.
- Cancellation or return rates are already high.
- The seller cannot dispatch quickly during a surge.

## 4. Break-even sales lift

If a discount is a fraction d of original price and original unit margin is a fraction m of original price (other costs fixed in rupees), then:

**Required sales lift = d ÷ (m − d)**

Example: price ₹500, cost ₹300 so m = 40%. Discount 10%: required lift = 0.10 ÷ (0.40 − 0.10) = 33.3%. Discount 20%: 0.20 ÷ 0.20 = 100%. Discount 30%: m − d = 0.10, lift = 300%. Discounts at or beyond m can never break even.

Check: before, 100 units × ₹200 = ₹20,000 margin. After 10% off, margin ₹150; 133.3 units needed to give ₹20,000.

If the marketplace fee is a percentage of price, the margin drop is slightly smaller; if shipping and packaging are fixed per order, it is larger. Use rupee contribution margins from KB-06 for accuracy.

## 4b. Promotion profitability checklist

1. Baseline orders per day before the promotion.
2. Expected promotional orders (a range, not a certainty).
3. Contribution margin per order at promotional price.
4. Stock cover for the expected orders.
5. Post-promotion dip allowance (pull-forward effect).
6. Extra returns or cancellations expected.
7. Total contribution compared with no-promotion scenario.

## 5. Promotional versus organic demand

Promotional demand is bought with margin. Measure the lift above the baseline, and check the dip afterwards.

Example (illustrative): baseline 10 orders/day, contribution ₹60 per order. Promotion for 7 days at 15% off gives 16 orders/day and contribution ₹20 per order.
- Baseline contribution over 7 days: 70 × 60 = ₹4,200.
- Promotional contribution: 112 × 20 = ₹2,240.
The promotion sold more units (112 vs 70) but earned ₹1,960 less. Unless it gains reviews, clears stock worth the value, or builds lasting demand, it did not pay.

## 6. Discount cannibalisation

Cannibalisation occurs when a discount mostly gives buyers who would have purchased at full price a cheaper deal. If 80% of promotional orders would have happened anyway, 80% of the discount is a pure loss.

Example: 100 baseline orders; promotion yields 120 orders at ₹50 lower price per unit. Discount cost on the 100 baseline = 100 × 50 = ₹5,000. Margin from 20 extra orders = 20 × (200 − 50) = ₹3,000. Net = −₹2,000 versus no promotion.

Reducing cannibalisation: target specific slow products, use bundles or volume offers (they raise basket size), and limit duration.

## 7. Bundling

A bundle combines items to raise order value and move slower items.

Example (illustrative): item A ₹500 (cost 300), item B ₹200 (cost 100). Bundle price ₹650 (discount ₹50, about 7%). Margin as bundle = 650 − 400 = ₹250 versus separately 300. Bundling only wins if buyers who would purchase just A now take A+B at a higher total, or if it clears B. Bundles need stock for both items; a stockout in one kills the bundle.

Risks: wrong combinations, increased packaging complexity, listing confusion.

## 8. Volume-based offers

"Buy 2 for ₹900" instead of ₹1,000 (10% off). If cost is ₹300, margin per unit pair falls from ₹400 to ₹300. It pays if many buyers who would take 1 now take 2. Check what share of buyers already buy multiple units.

## 9. Cross-selling and upselling

- Cross-sell: phone case with a screen guard; curtains with curtain rods; kurti with a dupatta.
- Upsell: a better-rated variant or a larger pack.
Requires complementary stock and good listings. Measure AOV and attach rate (orders containing both ÷ orders for the main item).

## 10. Trade-offs and risks

- Deep discounts can lower perceived quality and raise returns from price-driven buyers.
- Pulling demand forward can leave weaker weeks.
- A promotion on a product with poor reviews may increase negative feedback.
- Over-promising stock leads to cancellations and trust damage.
- Frequent promotions teach customers to wait.

## 11. Questions a KAM should ask

- What is the goal: clear stock, launch, or raise volume?
- What is your margin per order at the promotional price?
- How many units do you have, and how fast can you pack?
- Did your last promotion earn more or less contribution than normal days?
- Is price the reason people are not buying, or is it something else?

## 12. What not to conclude

- Do not promise a lift percentage.
- Do not recommend a promotion when stock is short.
- Do not assume every sales spike was caused by the promotion.
