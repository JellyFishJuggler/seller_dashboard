# Profitability and Unit Economics

This document explains how to check whether a marketplace seller actually makes money per order, per product and in total. Every cost figure is a **hypothetical assumption**, chosen to show the method. Real marketplace fees, commissions, shipping charges and penalties differ and must come from the seller's own statements or official platform documentation; no Snapdeal-specific fee is stated here (see KB-24).

## 1. Revenue versus profit

Revenue (or sales) is money billed. Profit is what remains after all costs. A seller can grow sales and still lose money if each extra order earns little or negative contribution. Cash is a third thing: stock bought in advance ties up cash even when sales are growing.

## 2. Costs: variable versus fixed

- **Variable costs** change with each order: product purchase cost, marketplace fees and commission (if percentage-based), shipping the seller pays, packaging, payment-related charges, return handling, ad cost per order.
- **Fixed costs** do not change with each order: rent, salaries, software, equipment, certain services.

## 3. Gross margin and contribution margin

- Gross margin = (Price − product cost) ÷ Price.
- Contribution margin per order = Price − all variable costs.

Contribution margin shows what each order contributes toward fixed costs and profit. It is the key number for discount, promotion and stock decisions.

## 4. Worked order-level example (hypothetical assumptions)

Assumptions: selling price ₹500; product cost ₹300; marketplace fee assumed 15% of price = ₹75; shipping paid by seller assumed ₹50; packaging assumed ₹15.

- Gross margin = (500 − 300) ÷ 500 = 40%.
- Contribution margin = 500 − 300 − 75 − 50 − 15 = ₹60, i.e. 12% of price.

The gross margin of 40% looks comfortable; the true per-order contribution is only 12%.

## 5. Adding returns (hypothetical)

Assume 10% of orders are returned, and each returned order costs the seller: forward shipping already paid ₹50 and no contribution, reverse shipping ₹50, plus 30% of product cost written off as unsellable (₹90). Loss per returned order = 50 + 50 + 90 = ₹190 (ignoring any fee refund).

Expected contribution per order placed = 90% × 60 − 10% × 190 = 54 − 19 = ₹35.

Returns cut contribution from ₹60 to ₹35 in this example. Each 1 point of return rate costs about ₹2.5 per order under these assumptions. See KB-15.

## 6. Break-even calculation

Break-even orders per month = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin per order.

With fixed costs assumed ₹20,000 per month:
- At ₹60 contribution: 20,000 ÷ 60 = 333.3, so at least 334 orders.
- At ₹35 (with returns): 20,000 ÷ 35 = 571.4, so at least 572 orders.

Higher return rates raise break-even volume sharply.

## 7. Minimum viable selling price

Minimum price to cover variable costs (zero contribution), when the fee is a percentage f of price:

P = (Product cost + shipping + packaging) ÷ (1 − f)

With cost 300, shipping 50, packaging 15, f = 15%: P = 365 ÷ 0.85 = ₹429.41. Check: fee = 64.41; 429.41 − 64.41 = 365.

To earn a target contribution of 10% of price: P = 365 ÷ (1 − 0.15 − 0.10) = 365 ÷ 0.75 = ₹486.67. Check: 486.67 × 0.75 = 365.

Below the minimum viable price each order loses money before fixed costs and returns.

## 8. Profitability of discounts

Discount 10% (₹500 to ₹450), fee still 15% (₹67.50):
Contribution = 450 − 300 − 67.50 − 50 − 15 = ₹17.50 (3.9% of price), versus ₹60 before.

Order volume needed to earn the same total contribution: 60 ÷ 17.5 = 3.43 times the old volume (an increase of about 243%). A 10% price cut here needs a very large sales lift because fixed per-order costs (shipping, packaging) do not shrink.

## 9. Product-level economics

Compute contribution per unit for each top product. A seller often discovers that:
- A bestseller has a thin contribution; a slower product has a rich one.
- Heavy or fragile products have high shipping and damage costs.
- Low-priced items are hit hardest by fixed per-order costs.

Rank products by contribution margin and by total contribution (units × margin). Promote high total contribution products; reconsider products with negative contribution.

## 10. Why higher sales do not always mean higher profit

Reasons: deep discounts; higher return or cancellation rates on the new orders; fixed per-order costs; extra ad spend; stock write-offs; price-war volume with thin margin. Compare contribution margin before and after any growth push.

## 11. Questions a KAM should ask

- What is your product cost, and how do you get your shipping and fee charges per order?
- Do your reports show net payment per order after deductions?
- Which product earns you the most per order?
- What is your monthly fixed cost, roughly?
- What is your return rate on your top three products?

## 12. What not to conclude

- Do not state a seller's profit change percentage from sales data alone.
- Do not apply an assumed fee rate to a real seller; use their statements.
- Do not recommend discounts without a break-even check.
- Do not treat contribution margin as net profit; fixed costs still apply.
