# Marketplace Seller Business Fundamentals

This document explains how an online marketplace seller business works, how its parts depend on each other, and why a revenue number alone does not describe business health. It is general e-commerce knowledge. It does not describe Snapdeal-specific rules, fees or policies; see KB-24 for platform-specific information and its verification limits.

All figures labelled "example" are illustrative assumptions, not real marketplace data.

## 1. How a marketplace business works

A marketplace is a platform where many independent sellers list products and many buyers shop in one place. The marketplace supplies traffic, a catalogue, search, checkout, payments and (usually) logistics. The seller supplies products, inventory, pricing, packaging and often the first-mile handover to a courier.

Four parties are involved in almost every order:

- **Seller**: owns or sources the product, sets the price (within platform rules), keeps stock, packs and hands over the order.
- **Marketplace**: shows the product to buyers, collects payment, applies rules and fees, and arbitrates disputes.
- **Logistics partner (courier)**: picks up from the seller or a hub and delivers to the buyer, and carries returns back.
- **Customer (buyer)**: searches, compares, orders, receives, keeps or returns the product, and may leave a rating or review.

A seller does not fully control the sale. Visibility, delivery experience, payment methods and customer trust are shared with the marketplace and couriers. A good Key Account Manager (KAM) separates what the seller controls (price, stock, content, packing speed, product quality) from what the seller can only influence (search placement, courier performance, buyer behaviour).

## 2. How a product generates revenue

Revenue for a single product follows a chain:

1. The product is **listed** (catalogue entry with title, images, attributes, price, stock).
2. It gets **impressions** (shown in search results, category pages, recommendations).
3. Some impressions become **clicks** (buyers open the product page).
4. Some clicks become **orders** (buyers pay or choose cash on delivery).
5. Some orders are **fulfilled** (packed, dispatched, delivered).
6. Some delivered orders are **kept** (not returned or refused).

Net revenue is what survives the whole chain. A leak at any step reduces income even when every other step is healthy. This is why diagnosing a sales fall means walking the chain, not guessing one cause.

## 3. The core arithmetic: sales, orders, price, inventory

The most important relationship is:

**Sales = Orders × Average Selling Price (ASP)**

Orders themselves depend on traffic and conversion:

**Orders = Impressions × Click-through rate × Conversion rate** (roughly, when the funnel is measured consistently)

Inventory sets a ceiling:

**Orders that can be fulfilled ≤ Stock available**

Example (illustrative): A seller sells 400 orders at ASP ₹500. Sales = 400 × 500 = ₹2,00,000. If orders fall to 270 and ASP stays ₹500, sales = 270 × 500 = ₹1,35,000. Because ASP did not change, the entire ₹65,000 fall is explained by order volume, not by price. The reason orders fell still has to be found (stock, cancellations, visibility, conversion, demand, competition, seasonality).

## 4. Dependencies between operations and commerce

Seller performance is a system. Changing one lever moves others.

- **Pricing ↔ demand ↔ margin**: lower prices may lift orders but cut margin per order; higher prices may protect margin but lower conversion.
- **Inventory ↔ visibility ↔ orders**: out-of-stock products usually cannot be bought. Platforms commonly hide or de-emphasise unavailable listings, but how any specific platform treats them must be confirmed from that platform's documentation.
- **Fulfilment speed ↔ cancellations ↔ trust**: slow dispatch and stock mismatches produce seller-side cancellations, which hurt buyers' confidence and can attract platform penalties (confirm penalty rules with the platform).
- **Content accuracy ↔ returns ↔ profit**: a misleading listing can raise conversion and also raise returns; net effect is usually negative.
- **Promotions ↔ stock ↔ cash**: a discount campaign without stock cover creates cancellations; a campaign on a low-margin product can raise sales while losing money.

## 5. The seller business lifecycle

Most marketplace sellers pass through recognisable stages. The right advice differs by stage.

- **Onboarding and setup**: registration, tax and bank documents, first catalogue, first inventory. Main risks: poor listing quality, wrong categories, unrealistic stock updates.
- **Early traction**: first orders and first returns. Main risks: slow dispatch, no stock buffer, pricing guesswork, reacting to every complaint.
- **Growth**: more SKUs, more orders. Main risks: stockouts on bestsellers, cancellations, cash tied in slow inventory, quality drift.
- **Plateau or decline**: orders flatten or fall. Main risks: dependence on one product, competitor entry, rising returns, no new products.
- **Optimisation or reset**: pruning weak SKUs, fixing operations, rebuilding assortment. Main risk: cutting visible costs while ignoring the root cause.

A KAM should ask where the seller is in this lifecycle before recommending actions. A fix that suits a plateaued seller (new product launches) may be wrong for an early seller (fix fulfilment first).

## 6. Revenue growth versus a sustainable business

Growing revenue means selling more in rupees. A sustainable business means that growth is repeatable and leaves money and customer goodwill behind.

Signs that revenue growth is **not** sustainable:

- Sales rise because of deep discounts, and contribution margin per order falls or turns negative.
- Orders rise but cancellations and returns rise faster, so net delivered sales do not.
- One or two products supply most of the sales, and they are frequently out of stock.
- Working capital is locked in slow-moving stock while bestsellers run out.
- Ratings fall because of quality or packaging issues.

Signs of a more sustainable business:

- Delivered, kept sales grow, not just orders placed.
- Margin per order is stable or improving after all variable costs.
- Stock covers expected demand for the replenishment lead time.
- Cancellation and return reasons are understood and addressed.
- Revenue comes from several products and the seller can launch new ones.

Example (illustrative assumptions): Seller A grows orders 20% by discounting 15% on every product. If the average product margin before discount was 25% of price, the discount consumes 15 of those 25 points per sale (roughly), so each extra order earns much less. Orders growing 20% may produce less total profit than before. A KAM should not celebrate the order growth without checking margin (see KB-06 and KB-07).

## 7. Common mistakes made by marketplace sellers

- Watching only sales and ignoring orders, ASP, cancellations and returns separately.
- Leaving stock counts out of sync with the physical warehouse, causing cancellations.
- Copying competitor titles and images that do not match their own product.
- Cutting price first when the real issue is availability, content or fulfilment.
- Running promotions without checking break-even sales lift.
- Not tracking which products drive most of the revenue.
- Restocking all out-of-stock items equally instead of prioritising by demand.
- Ignoring recurring complaint themes in returns and reviews.
- Treating every problem as the marketplace's fault, or every problem as the seller's fault, without evidence.
- Changing many things at once so that no result can be attributed to a cause.

## 8. Practical marketplace examples

**Example 1: home decor seller (illustrative).** Orders fall after the seller switches to thinner packaging to save cost. Orders were fine, but damage-related returns rise and ratings drop. The apparent saving per parcel is smaller than the lost revenue and extra reverse-logistics cost. Lesson: cost cuts can damage downstream metrics.

**Example 2: mobile accessories seller (illustrative).** A charging cable listing gets many clicks but returns are high because the title does not state the connector type. Fixing content, not price, is the right action.

**Example 3: apparel seller (illustrative).** A top-selling kurti runs out of sizes M and L but still has stock in XXL. The product looks "in stock" at the product level while its demand-heavy sizes are unavailable. Product-level stock counts can hide size-level stockouts (see KB-18).

## 9. Questions a KAM should ask

- Which products make most of your sales, and are they all in stock right now?
- Are your stock numbers on the panel updated each day from your real stock?
- Did anything change in the last month: price, packaging, supplier, courier, product mix, a promotion?
- Are your orders falling because fewer people buy, or because orders are being cancelled?
- How are you dispatching: do you pack the same day, and who handles it?
- Which products give you the most profit per order, not just the most orders?

## 10. What not to conclude

- Do not conclude that a sales fall is a pricing problem when ASP is unchanged; check order volume drivers first.
- Do not conclude that stockouts or cancellations caused the entire decline without product-level history; they are hypotheses until checked.
- Do not claim marketplace-specific rules (fees, penalties, ranking logic, payout timelines) from general knowledge; confirm from official documentation (KB-24).
- Do not treat order growth as business health without cancellation, return and margin data.
