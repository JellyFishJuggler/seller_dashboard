# Product Assortment and Seller Growth

This document explains how a marketplace seller should manage the range of products sold: identify bestsellers, handle the long tail, reduce concentration risk, launch products and expand into categories. All numbers are illustrative.

## 1. Product portfolio management

Think of the catalogue as a portfolio. Different products play different roles:
- **Core bestsellers**: bring most sales; must stay in stock.
- **Supporting products**: steady, modest sales; often good margin.
- **Traffic or entry products**: attract buyers at low price; thin margin.
- **Complementary products**: raise order value through cross-sell.
- **Experimental new products**: unproven; small stock.
- **Problem products**: low sales, high returns or poor margin; fix or exit.

## 2. Bestseller identification

Rank products by orders, sales value and contribution margin over a period that matches seasonality. A true bestseller scores well on all three or at least on total contribution. Distinguish a bestseller from a product that sells because of a one-off promotion.

## 3. Long-tail products

The long tail is the many products with small sales each. They add choice but also cost: stock, listing upkeep, working capital and operational complexity. Review the tail periodically:
- Keep if it has positive contribution, low holding cost or strategic value (completes a range).
- Improve if it has potential (content, price).
- Exit if it consumes cash or attention with no contribution.

## 4. Product concentration risk

If a small number of products generate most sales, one stockout, quality issue or competitor can hurt heavily.

Share of top 3 = (sales of top three) ÷ total sales. Example: ₹1,20,000 of ₹2,00,000 = 60%. Lowering concentration means building second and third strong products, not necessarily cutting the top ones.

Warning signs: top product out of stock; one supplier for all bestsellers; rapid growth of one product's share.

## 5. Prioritising existing products versus new products

Rule of thumb for a seller with limited time and cash:
1. First secure the availability, quality and listing of existing bestsellers.
2. Fix problem products that drain margin.
3. Improve mid-performing products with clear potential.
4. Add new products only when operations are stable (stock accuracy, low cancellation, manageable returns).

Adding new products while core products are out of stock spreads cash and attention thin. For the sample seller (stockouts rose from 5 to 18), restoring availability for existing demand is a stronger first step than launching new products, but only if product-level history confirms the stocked-out items are demand-heavy.

## 5b. Questions for the new-versus-existing decision

- Are my top products in stock and healthy?
- Do I have capacity to handle more SKUs?
- Is there evidence of demand for the new product (search, competitors' sales, buyer questions)?
- Can I test with a small quantity?

## 6. Product launches

A launch plan:
1. Choose a product with a clear buyer need, acceptable margin after all costs (KB-06), and adequate supplier reliability.
2. Prepare complete, accurate listing and images.
3. Start with limited stock based on conservative demand.
4. Monitor impressions, clicks, conversion, returns and feedback for several weeks.
5. Decide: scale, adjust or stop.

Avoid launching many items at once because problems cannot be traced.

## 7. Category expansion

Expansion into a new category brings new rules, return patterns, packaging needs and competition. Start with a category adjacent to the seller's strengths (similar buyers, similar supply chain). Check category-specific requirements and fees from official sources (KB-24). Test small.

## 8. Cross-selling and assortment planning

Create products that pair with bestsellers (accessories, add-ons, refills). Plan assortment depth (variants) and breadth (categories) by demand evidence. Keep variants only if they sell.

## 9. Product lifecycle

Introduction → growth → maturity → decline. Match actions to stage: invest and stock in growth; protect margin in maturity; clear and phase out in decline. Seasonal products have repeated mini-cycles.

## 10. Growth planning

Plan growth along several levers:
- Availability (stock cover on winners).
- Conversion and listing quality.
- Visibility.
- Price and margin management.
- Range extension.
- Operational capacity.
Set growth targets in contribution, not only in sales; track each lever with a metric.

## 11. Questions a KAM should ask

- Which products give you most of your sales and most of your profit?
- Which products haven't sold in 90 days?
- Do you depend on one supplier or one product?
- Are you planning new products? How will you test them?
- Do you have the capacity to handle more orders?

## 12. What not to conclude

- Do not recommend dropping a product from sales data alone; check margin and role.
- Do not recommend large new launches when core stock or operations are unstable.
- Do not promise growth outcomes.
